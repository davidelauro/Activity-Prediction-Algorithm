# What makes a place feel socially alive

Run 2026-10-09 via a fresh subagent (Claude Opus), independent of the main project conversation,
same discipline as the other searches: every claim graded by how much was actually verified.

## How to read it

- **[FULL]** The full text was obtained and read.
- **[RECORD]** The publisher or repository record was retrieved; abstract-level claims verified,
  full text not read.
- **[LEAD]** Seen only via search results or secondhand citation. Citation details may be wrong,
  treat as a pointer to check, not evidence.

## 1. Density and crowding splits into two constructs with opposite signs

**Human crowding** (perceived presence of other people) and **spatial crowding** (perceived
constraint of physical space) behave oppositely. Blut and Iyer (2020), a meta-analysis of 73
samples and over 19,000 shoppers, *Journal of Retailing* 96(3):362-382, doi:10.1016/j.jretai.2019.11.007
[RECORD]: spatial crowding has negative outcomes, mediated by reduced perceived control; human
crowding has *positive* effects on evaluation and does not reduce perceived control. Origin of
the distinction: Machleit, Kellaris, and Eroglu (1994), *Marketing Letters* 5(2):183-194
[RECORD].

One direct test of an inverted-U specifically on human crowding: Cheng, Liu, and Bi (2021),
*Tourism Management Perspectives* 40:100888 [LEAD, fetch blocked], 555 questionnaires at Chinese
festivals, found an inverted-U between perceived human crowding and festival experience, while
spatial crowding stayed monotonically negative. This is the only direct quadratic test found; a
genuine gap, not a settled result.

## 2. Known crowd versus novelty: familiarity has the stronger support

Dunbar et al. (2017), *Adaptive Human Behavior and Physiology* 3(2):118-133,
doi:10.1007/s40750-016-0058-4 [FULL, read in full] is the strongest single result here. An
observational study contrasting small community pubs against large city-centre bars found: mean
group size 3.94 (regulars at their local) versus 6.73 (casual visitors, city bars); conversation
group size held steady around 3.44 across venue types; but in large/high-turnover venues,
significantly more people were physically present in a conversation without participating,
spent more time not talking, and conversations lasted significantly shorter. **Bigger,
higher-turnover venues produce larger parties but less actual talking.**

A separate, well-specified concept for novelty/turnover rather than familiarity: Hristova et al.
(2016), WWW '16, doi:10.1145/2872427.2883065 [LEAD], defines four computable place-level
metrics, social brokerage, entropy, visitor homogeneity, and serendipity, to separate places
that bring strangers together from places that bring the same friends together. No study found
pits familiarity and turnover against each other as competing predictors of perceived liveliness
specifically; that comparison looks genuinely open.

## 3. Dwell time is both a measure of liveliness and an effect of it

In urban design, liveliness is often *operationally defined* as stationary activity plus
duration of stay (Mehta 2007, *Journal of Planning Education and Research* 27(2):165-187
[RECORD]), which makes it circular as an input unless its role is stated explicitly. Separately,
dwell time rises with group size (Bell and Pliner 2003, *Appetite* 41(2):215-218 [LEAD]):
meal duration correlates positively with group size across three settings. Dwell time is
endogenous to group size, not an independent driver.

## 4. Visible conversation and synchrony

Dunbar et al. (2017), as above, gives directly usable proxies: fraction of present people in
active conversation, mean conversation-group size (around 3.4), conversation duration. Tarr et
al. (2015), *Biology Letters* [LEAD], found synchronized movement (dancing) and physical
exertion each independently raise social bonding in a controlled study, the best support found
for visible synchrony as a real driver rather than a correlate.

## 5. Noise and music: volume raises consumption but suppresses the conversation that signals liveliness

Louder music increases drinks ordered and drinking speed (Gueguen et al. 2008, *Alcoholism:
Clinical and Experimental Research* [LEAD, unverified page range]), but the Lombard effect,
involuntary rise in vocal effort with background noise, means a loud room becomes
self-amplifying and conversation degrades. Restaurant-acoustics work found willingness to spend
time and money falls as background noise rises. No study found estimates an optimal noise level
directly; theorized, not measured.

## 6. Soundscape "vibrancy": the one framework that makes "lively" a measured variable

Axelsson, Nilsson, and Berglund (2010), *Journal of the Acoustical Society of America*
128(5):2836-2846, doi:10.1121/1.3493436 [RECORD]: 100 listeners rated 50 urban soundscapes
across 116 attributes, reducing to three components, Pleasantness (50% of variance),
Eventfulness (18%), Familiarity (6%). Soundscapes dominated by human sounds rated as eventful
independent of loudness. The ISO 12913 soundscape standard places "vibrant" at pleasant +
eventful on a circumplex, with "chaotic" at eventful without pleasant, the same eventfulness,
opposite outcome. This is probably the best-specified existing answer to "what makes a place
feel lively": audible human presence driving eventfulness, moderated by whether it stays
pleasant.

## 7. Collective effervescence: a caution against crowd-size-centric models

Gabriel et al. (2020), *Journal of Positive Psychology* 15(1):129-154,
doi:10.1080/17439760.2019.1689412 [FULL, read in part], validated a State Collective
Effervescence scale across nine studies and over 2,500 participants. In one regression (Study
2d): number of people present had only a small, significant relation to the Connection
subscale (beta = 0.16) and no significant relation to Sacredness; number of friends present was
non-significant for both. Event enjoyment, positive mood, and flow dominated instead. Not
something to over-generalize from a single regression, but a real caution against treating
headcount as the primary driver of felt social aliveness.

## 8. Crowd as a visible signal to people outside it

Whyte's classic concept, people are drawn to where other people already are ("self-congestion"),
plus observational-learning effects where uninformed people infer quality from observed demand
(the "empty restaurant" effect). This is the mechanism behind the feedback loop already flagged
in `drafts/literature-search-topics.md` (question 8): a visible crowd changes future arrivals,
which is a different question from what makes a crowd feel alive to the people already in it.

## What the literature does not settle

Stated plainly by the search itself, and worth taking at face value: no single study jointly
tests density, familiarity, dwell time, conversation, and sound against one perceived-liveliness
outcome; they live in separate literatures with separate outcome variables. The sweet spot on
crowding is theorized more than measured. Turnover as a driver of felt liveliness (as opposed to
encounter diversity) is essentially untested. Dwell time's role as input versus outcome needs to
be stated explicitly in any model that uses it.

**The two anchors the search itself called most defensible:** Blut and Iyer (2020) for the
human/spatial crowding split, and Axelsson et al. (2010) plus the ISO 12913 circumplex for
"lively" as pleasant times eventful, with eventfulness driven by audible human presence.
